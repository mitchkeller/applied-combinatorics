    Activity 5.1.

    Suppose \(\bfH\) is an induced spanning subgraph of a graph \(\bfG\text{.}\) Discuss with your group what this would mean.
    🔗
    Solution.
    Here we must have that \(\bfG\) and \(\bfH\) are the same graph. The spanning property implies that \(\bfH\) has all of the vertices of \(\bfG\text{.}\) Being induced then forces all of the edges of \(\bfG\) to be in \(\bfH\) since \(\bfH\) contains all edges of \(\bfG\) that have both their endpoints in \(\bfH\text{,}\) which has all of the vertices of \(\bfG\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Two graphs
    🔗
    These graphs are isomorphic. One possible isomorphism is shown below.
    🔗
    \(v\) \(a\) \(b\) \(c\) \(d\) \(e\) \(f\)
    \(f(v)\) \(1\) \(3\) \(5\) \(6\) \(2\) \(4\)
    
    Two graphs
    🔗
    These graphs are not isomorphic. To see why they are not, we try to construct an isomorphism. Since \(a\) and \(1\) are the only vertices of degree \(1\text{,}\) any isomorphism would have to pair them. This then forces \(c\) and \(2\text{,}\) as the unique neighbors of the vertices of degree \(1\) to be paired. However, all neighbors of \(c\) have degree \(3\text{,}\) while \(2\) has a neighbor (\(4\)) of degree \(2\text{.}\) Another independent reason would be that \(f\) and \(4\) are the unique vertices of degree \(2\) in these graphs, so they would have to be paired. However, \(f\) has two neighbors of degree \(3\text{,}\) while \(4\) has a neighbor of degree \(4\text{.}\) As a third independent reason, notice that in the left graph, the vertex of degree \(1\) and the vertex of degree \(2\) are distance 3 apart. However, in the right graph, the vertex of degree \(1\) and the vertex of degree \(2\) are distance 2 apart.

    On the Complexity of Graph Isomorphism
    🔗
    The question we would like to answer algorithmically is “Given two graphs \(\bfG\) and \(\bfH\text{,}\) is \(\bfG\) isomorphic to \(\bfH\text{?}\)” The two most-studied classes of computational problems are \(\bfP\) and \(\bfNP\text{,}\) with a focus on \(\bfNP\)-complete problems. The problems in \(\bfP\) have efficient algorithms, by which we roughly mean the number of operations an algorithm takes is polynomial in the size of the input (usually number of vertices for graphs). The \(\bfNP\)-complete problems are believed to be computationally intractable but must all be of a type where a yes or no answer can be readily verified. (For instance, if I give you a function that I claim is an isomorphism, it’s fast to verify this fact.) Most problems that fit this framework have been shown to be in \(\bfP\) or to be \(\bfNP\)-complete. However, graph isomorphism has not shown to be either. In fact, it is often considered to be the leading candidate for a problem that is neither in \(\bfP\) nor \(\bfNP\)-complete.
    🔗

    Theorem 5.2. The First Theorem of Graph Theory aka The Handshaking Lemma.

    Let \(\deg_{\bfG}(v)\) denote the degree of vertex \(v\) in graph \(\GVE\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \sum_{v\in V}\deg_{\bfG}(v) = 2|E|\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    The summation counts an edge \(xy\) twice: once when \(v=x\) and once when \(v=y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} |E| = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{v\in V}\deg_{\bfG}(v) \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Corollary 5.3.

    In any graph, the number of vertices of odd degree is even.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    Adding up an odd number of odd numbers gives an odd number, and the sum would remain odd no matter how many even numbers are added in.

    Definition 5.4.

    • A tree is a connected graph with no cycles.

    Every tree on at least two vertices has at least two leaves.

    Theorem 5.6.

    If \(\bfT\) is a tree, then for every pair of distinct vertices \(u,v\text{,}\) there exists a unique path from \(u\) to \(v\) in \(\bfT\text{.}\)

    Activity 5.7.

    1. With your neighbors, use mathematical induction to prove that every tree on \(n\) vertices has exactly \(n-1\) edges.
      🔗
    2. How many edges would an \(n\)-vertex forest consisting of \(k\) trees have?
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.

    Proof.

    For the base case, when \(n=1\text{,}\) the tree has only one vertex and thus no edges. Since \(n-1 = 1-1=0\text{,}\) we have the desired relationship. Now assume that for some \(k\geq 1\text{,}\) every tree with \(k\) vertices has exactly \(k-1\) edges. Let \(\bfT\) be a tree with \(k+1\) vertices. The tree \(\bfT\) has a leaf \(v\text{.}\) Form \(\bfT'\) from \(\bfT\) by deleting \(v\) and its edge. Then \(\bfT'\) has \(k\) vertices, so by the induction hypothesis, \(\bfT'\) has \(k-1\) edges. However, \(\bfT\) has one more edge (incident with \(v\)) than \(\bfT'\text{,}\) so \(\bfT\) has \(k = k+1-1\) edges.
    🔗
    🔗
    Notice in the proof above that it was essential to delete a leaf, as otherwise we would not know that the new graph is still a tree (it might be a forest).
    🔗
    A forest is a graph with no cycles, meaning that each component of a forest is itself a tree. Thus, we would need to have \(n-k\) edges.
