    Handout 8.2 Partitions and Newton’s Binomial Theorem
    

    Partitions of Integers.

    Definition 8.4.

    A partition of the positive integer \(n\) is a way of writing \(n\) as a sum of nonincreasing positive integers.
    🔗
    🔗
    \(4+2+2+1+1\) is a partition of \(10\text{.}\) We also note that \(10\) is a partition of \(10\text{.}\) However, we do not count \(4+2+1+2+1\) as a partition of \(10\) because the terms in the sum are not in nonincreasing order.
    🔗

    Definition 8.5.

    A partition of a positive integer is said to be a partition into odd parts if every term (also referred to as a part) in the sum is odd.
    🔗
    🔗
    \(3+1+1\) is a partition of \(5\) into odd parts. Also note that \(5\) is a partition of \(5\) into odd parts.
    🔗

    Definition 8.6.

    A partition of a positive integer is said to be a partition into distinct parts if each integer appears in the sum at most once.
    🔗
    🔗
    \(3+1\) is a partition of \(4\) into distinct parts. \(5+2+1\) is a partition of \(8\) into distinct parts.
    🔗

    Activity 8.2.1.

    List the partitions of \(9\text{.}\) Count the number of partitions into odd parts. Count the number of partitions into distinct parts.
    🔗
    Hint.
    Be systematic! One way to do this is by grouping partitions by their largest part.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    \begin{align*} 9\amp \amp 3\amp +3+3\\ 8\amp +1 \amp 3\amp +3+2+1\\ 7\amp +2 \amp 3\amp +3+1+1+1\\ 7\amp +1+1 \amp 3\amp +2+2+2\\ 6\amp +3 \amp 3\amp +2+2+1+1\\ 6\amp +2+1 \amp 3\amp +2+1+1+1+1\\ 6\amp +1+1+1 \amp 3\amp +1+1+1+1+1+1\\ 5\amp +4 \amp 2\amp +2+2+2+1\\ 5\amp +3+1 \amp 2\amp +2+2+1+1+1\\ 5\amp +2+2 \amp 2\amp +2+1+1+1+1+1\\ 5\amp +2+1+1 \amp 2\amp +1+1+1+1+1+1+1\\ 5\amp +1+1+1+1 \amp 1\amp +1+1+1+1+1+1+1+1\\ 4\amp +4+1 \amp \\ 4\amp +3+2 \amp \\ 4\amp +3+1+1 \amp \\ 4\amp +2+2+1 \amp \\ 4\amp +2+1+1+1 \amp \\ 4\amp +1+1+1+1+1 \amp \end{align*}
    🔗
    There are 8 partitions into odd parts and 8 partitions into distinct parts.
    🔗
    How would we write a generating function in which the coefficient on \(x^n\) is the number of partitions of \(n\text{?}\)
    \begin{equation*} \left(\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^{2n}\right)\left(\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^{3n}\right)\cdots = \left(\frac{1}{1-x}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-x^2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{1-x^3}\right)\cdots = \prod_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1-x^k} \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Activity 8.2.2.

    (a)

    Write a generating function in which the coefficient on \(x^{n}\) is the number of partitions of \(n\) into distinct parts.
    🔗
    Hint.
    This is conveniently done as a product of simple generating functions.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\displaystyle \prod_{k=1}^\infty (1+x^k)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Write a generating function in which the coefficient on \(x^{n}\) is the number of partitions of \(n\) into odd parts.
    🔗
    Hint.
    A convenient form is a product of rational functions, but you might want to start with a product of power series and rewrite it.
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\displaystyle \prod_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{1-x^{2k+1}} = \prod_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1}{1-x^{2k-1}}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Show that your generating functions above are actually equal to one another.
    🔗
    Hint.
    \(\displaystyle (1+x^{k})\frac{1-x^{k}}{1-x^{k}}= \frac{\text{?}}{1-x^{k}}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\displaystyle \prod_{k=1}^\infty (1+x^k) = \prod_{k=1}^\infty \frac{1-x^{2k}}{1-x^k} = \prod_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{1-x^{2k+1}}\) by cancelling all the factors from the numerator with the factors in the denominator that have odd powers of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Newton’s Binomial Theorem.

    Definition 8.7.

    For all real numbers \(p\) and nonnegative integers \(k\text{,}\) the number \(P(p,k)\) is defined by
    1. \(P(p,0)=1\) for all real numbers \(p\) and
      🔗
      🔗
    2. \(P(p,k) = p P(p-1,k-1)\) for all real numbers \(p\) and integers \(k\gt 0\text{.}\)
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    \(P(5,3)=5\cdot 4\cdot 3\) based on what we did in Chapter 2. Here we can work it out and find \(P(5,3) = 5\cdot P(4,2) = 5\cdot 4\cdot P(3,1) = 5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot P(2,0) = 5\cdot 4\cdot 3\cdot 1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 8.8.

    For all real numbers \(p\) and nonnegative integers \(k\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} \binom{p}{k}=\frac{P(p,k)}{k!}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 8.2.3.

    Compute \(\displaystyle\binom{-9/2}{5}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \(\displaystyle \binom{-9/2}{5} = \frac{P(-9/2,5)}{5!} = \frac{1}{5!}\left(\frac{-9}{2}\right) \left(\frac{-11}{2}\right) \left(\frac{-13}{2}\right) \left(\frac{-15}{2}\right) \left(\frac{-17}{2}\right) = -\frac{1}{5!} \frac{1}{2^5} (9\cdot 11 \cdot 13\cdot 15\cdot 17)\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 8.9. Newton’s Binomial Theorem.

    For all real \(p\) with \(p\neq0\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} (1+x)^{p}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom{p}{n}x^{n}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Lemma 8.10.

    For each \(k\geq 0\text{,}\) \(P(p,k+1)=P(p,k)(p-k)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 8.2.4.

    (a)
    Use mathematical induction to show that for all \(k\geq 0\text{,}\) \(\displaystyle \binom{-1/2}{k}= (-1)^{k}\frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{2^{2k}}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    (b)
    Use Newton’s Binomial Theorem and the step above to write \((1-4x)^{-1/2}\) as a formal power series in which the coefficient on \(x^{n}\) is a binomial coefficient in which both numbers are integers.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} (1-4x)^{-1/2} \amp = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{-1/2}{k} (-4x)^k = \sum_{k=0}^\infty (-1)^k \frac{\binom{2k}{k}}{2^{2k}}(-1)^k 4^k x^k\\ \amp = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{2k}{k} x^k \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Recall that the Catalan numbers are \(\displaystyle\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\text{,}\) so the generating function above is very close. If we integrate both sides of the solution to the activity above, we get
    \begin{align*} \int (1-4x)^{-1/2}\, dx \amp = \int\sum_{k=0}^\infty \binom{2k}{k}x^k\, dx\\ -\frac{1}{2}(1-4x)^{1/2} + C\amp = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}x^{k+1} + D = x\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}x^k + D \end{align*}
    I’ll leave the tinkering with the constants of integration to another day.
