    Worksheet 9.1 Solving Linear, Constant Coefficient Homogeneous Recurrence Equations
    

    These activities will help you develop methods for solving advancement operator equations. Recall that these are convenient ways of writing recurrence relations. Our focus at first will be on homogeneous, linear, constant coefficient equations. This activity concludes with an initial investigation of the nonhomogeneous (linear, constant coefficient) case, which we will explore further in a future activity. Nonlinear recurrences and those with nonconstant coefficients require additional, advanced techniques.
    🔗

    Activity 9.1.1. Degree two, no other roots.

    (a)

    Verify that \(f_{2}(n) = c_{2}n3^{n}\) is a solution of \((A-3)^{2}f=0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Hint.
    To do this, you need to evaluate \((A-3)^{2}[c_{2}n3^{n}]\) and simplify fully. I recommend doing this by first evaluating \((A-3)[c_{2}n3^{n}]\text{.}\) When you simplify that, you should get \(3c_{2}3^{n}\text{,}\) but do the work and make sure you get this simplified form. Then apply \((A-3)\) to \(3c_{2}3^{n}\) and simplify. You should get \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} (A-3)^2[c_2n3^n] \amp=(A-3)[c_2(n+1)3^{n+1}-3c_2n3^n]\\ \amp=(A-3)[3c_23^n]\\ \amp=3c_23^{n+1}-3\cdot 3c_23^n = 0 \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Determine if \(f_{3}(n) = c_{3} n^{2}3^{n}\) is a solution of \((A-3)^{2}f=0\text{.}\) If it’s not, what do you get instead of \(0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{align*} (A-3)^2[c_3n^23^n] \amp= (A-3)[c_3(n+1)^23^{n+1}-3c_3n^23^n]\\ \amp=(A-3)[6c_3 n3^n+3c_33^n]\\ \amp= 6c_3(n+1)3^{n+1}+3c_33^{n+1} - 3\cdot 6c_3n3^n-9c_33^n\\ \amp= 9c_33^n \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Since we already know that \(f_{1}(n) = c_{1} 3^{n}\) is a solution of \((A-3)f=0\) (and thus \((A-3)^{2}f=0\)), adding it to \(f_{2}\) gives a two parameter family of solutions. Find \(c_{1}\) and \(c_{2}\) so that \(f(n) = c_{1}3^{n}+c_{2}n3^{n}\) is a solution of \((A-3)^{2}f=0\) if \(f(0)=1\) and \(f(1) = 4\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    We find that \(f(0)=c_{1}\) and \(f(1) = 3c_{1} + 3c_{2}\text{.}\) We know the former is \(1\text{,}\) so \(c_{1}=1\text{.}\) Since \(f(1)=4\) and \(c_{1} = 1\text{,}\) we see that \(4=3+3c_{2}\text{.}\) Hence \(c_{2} = 1/3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 9.1.2. Repeated root of degree two, other roots.

    (a)

    Now consider the advancement operator equation \((A+2)(A-3)^{2}f=0\text{.}\) Use part 9.1.1.c and what you learned in the class prep to propose a general solution to this equation.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Verify that your solution works. You might think about how you can divide and conquer this verification work amongst your group. Note that advancement operator polynomials are linear transformations and you can commute the factors in an advancement operator polynomial so that applying \((A-3)^{2}(A+2)\) will give the same result as applying \((A+2)(A-3)^{2}\text{.}\) This fact is not true of all linear transformations—matrix multiplication is not commutative, after all! However, it works here.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 9.1.3. Higher degrees, no other roots.

    (a)

    Consider the advancement operator equation \((A-5)^{3}f=0\text{.}\) Conjecture what terms should be in its solution.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Verify your conjecture from the previous step.
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Apply \((A-5)\) to \(f_{4}(n) = c_{4} n^{4} 5^{n}\text{.}\) What’s the highest power of \(n\) remaining? How many more times would you need to apply \((A-5)\) to finally get \(0\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Apply \((A-5)\) to \(f_{5}(n) = c_{5} n^{5} 5^{n}\text{.}\) What’s the highest power of \(n\) remaining?
    🔗
    🔗

    (e)

    Conjecture what the highest power of \(n\) is that remains when you apply \((A-5)\) to \(f_{k}(n) = c_{k} n^{k} 5^{n}\text{.}\) (Here \(k\geq 0\) is an integer.)
    🔗
    🔗

    (f)

    Use the Binomial Theorem to explain why your conjecture in the previous step is correct.
    🔗
    🔗

    (g)

    What value of \(m\) makes \((A-5)^{m} f_{k}(n) =0\) for the function \(f_{k}\) from part 9.1.3.e? We say here that \((A-5)^{m}\) annihilates \(f_{k}(n)\) because applying the operator to \(f_{k}\) yields \(0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 9.1.5. Working toward the non-homogeneous case.

    (a)

    What happens if you apply \((A-3)\) to the function \(f(n) = 5^{n}\text{?}\) (Write your answer as a multiple of \(5^{n}\text{.}\))
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    Now suppose we’re trying to solve \((A+2)(A-3)f=5^{n}\text{?}\) (Look, ma, the right side isn’t zero!)
    🔗
    (i)
    What does the previous step suggest might be a function you could apply \((A+2)(A-3)\) to while retaining a power of \(5\) afterward?
    🔗
    🔗
    (ii)
    Apply \((A+2)(A-3)\) to your function. Write the result as a multiple of \(5^{n}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    (iii)
    You want this result to be just \(5^{n}\text{.}\) What do you need to do to achieve this?
    🔗
    Hint.
    Do you have an arbitrary constant remaining in the result? If not, how could you tweak the function you tried to get a constant in your result?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    (iv)
    What can you add to your function \(f\) without affecting the fact that \((A+2)(A-3)f=5^{n}\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Suppose now that you’re trying to solve \((A+2)(A-3)f=3^{n}\text{.}\) What would you need to try in order to preserve the \(3^{n}\) when applying \((A+2)(A-3)\text{?}\)
